Combat Prototype

Role Solo Game & Systems Designer
Engine & Tools Unity (URP), C#, ScriptableObjects
Status Design Document — Pre-production
Currently in design. This is an original systems-design document I'm authoring for an SAO-inspired co-op action combat game, ahead of any prototype build. It's a personal design exercise in replacing hotbar combat with execution-driven combat, and in designing raid encounters that stay unpredictable without feeling unfair.

Vision

The core pitch: pressing a button to trigger a pre-scripted ability is boring. This design instead makes the player's own input skill — directional aiming, timed holds, typed or spoken commands — the thing that determines the outcome of an attack, closer to Kingdom Come: Deliverance's combat than a traditional MMO hotbar. The project started as a full always-on MMORPG concept; after weighing what's actually buildable at indie scale, I narrowed the target to session-based co-op (2–8 players, matchmade or friends-only instanced encounters with a persistent crafting hub) — structurally closer to Monster Hunter or Deep Rock Galactic than to WoW. The core combat and co-op mechanics below were designed to support that scope without any redesign, keeping a larger persistent world open as a future expansion rather than the starting bet.

Three Combat Styles, One Philosophy

Every weapon class is designed around the same idea — skill expression comes from execution, not from a rotation — but each style earns that skill expression differently.

Melee — Directional & Sword Arts

Lock-on reads directional input relative to the target, not the camera. Holding an attack for ~2 seconds charges an uninterruptible Sword Art. Blocking is either omnidirectional (Sword & Shield, Greatsword) or directional, For Honor-style (Rapier, single-hand sword) — the same directional guard that powers the co-op Guard Switch.

Archery — Loadout-Driven Ranged

Free-aim, no QTEs — an early QTE-based draft was cut because it tested reflexes, not aim. Drawing is an analog hold with stamina-based sway, so holding indefinitely doesn't guarantee a clean shot. A three-layer loadout (bow chassis, modular components, ammo effects) gives archery its own build space, positioned as a glass cannon: highest raw damage, no personal block, genuinely dependent on a melee ally for cover.

Magic — Voice & Text Casting

Spells are constructed live by combining recognized keywords (base elements + modifiers), spoken or typed, parsed against a modular C# keyword dictionary. Casting roots the player in place, matching an FFXIV/WoW hard-cast bar — a damage-threshold interrupt system, plus mid-fight spell storage, turns that vulnerability into a real risk/reward loop.

Co-op: The Switch Mechanic

The centerpiece cooperative system, and the part of the design I'm most invested in: deliberate, physical two-player coordination rather than a passive stat buff. Compared to a solo Dodge (full avoidance, precise personal timing) or Block (partial mitigation, forgiving timing), a Switch trades individual execution skill for team-coordination skill — harder to land, bigger payoff, and it always grants a guaranteed aggro steal.

  • Guard Switch (reactive): a tanking or casting ally calls for help against an attack they can't survive or that would interrupt their cast; a second player must react within a timing window and guard in the matching direction to intercept it.
  • Vanguard Switch (proactive): a flanking ally dashes in and, once in range, gets grabbed and thrown into position by the player up front — a repositioning/engage tool rather than an emergency save.

Raid Design: Controlled Randomness

A direct response to a critique I have of FFXIV/WoW/Guild Wars 2-style raid design: fights that are fully memorizable become a spreadsheet exercise rather than a live read of the boss. Bosses here randomly select from a pool of viable attacks instead of following a fixed script, while target selection stays logical and readable (highest aggro, or furthest player) — unpredictable, not unfair. Gear also shifts away from boss drop tables toward crafting materials, making dedicated crafter players load-bearing to progression rather than optional — which raises its own design problem (a rare drop is useless if the right crafter is offline) that I'm resolving with an asynchronous commission-board system.

Open Design Questions

Documenting what isn't settled is as much a part of this GDD as what is. A few of the open threads I'm actively iterating on:

  • Whether Determination (the penalty for using an off-proficiency weapon) is raised through general stat leveling or per-weapon proficiency gained from use.
  • Whether a Sword Art roots the player in place while it executes, or allows movement.
  • Exact values for the boss stagger/break meter, and whether melee, archery, and magic contribute to it equally.
  • How magic's keyword parsing should adapt to languages with different word order (German, Japanese, Simplified Chinese) for localization.
  • Whether the first playable prototype should be a whitebox dummy room or a small scripted boss encounter.
